## Strategic Planning Assumptions

By 2028, organizations that model and watch inventory decisions will report much lower stockout and overstock losses. They will beat organizations that still rely on fixed reorder rules and manual cross-checks.

By 2027, most mid-market FMCG and logistics firms will adopt AI-driven forecasting or allocation. Today, only a few do.

By 2029, "inventory intelligence" will be its own capability. People will view it the way they now view "data literacy" or "supply chain resilience." It won't be seen as just a software feature.

Through 2027, the main barrier won't be tech cost. It will be trust. Planners and category managers need to trust system recommendations as much as their own judgment.

## What Inventory Intelligence Is

Inventory intelligence is the practice of managing stock levels, replenishment, and allocation across a multi-tier supply chain. It uses nonstop, unified visibility and predictive, AI-assisted decision support. Most organizations still rely on old, backward-looking reports instead.

The term is often misused, so let's clarify. Inventory intelligence doesn't just mean having a dashboard. It isn't a product you buy off a shelf. It's a capability an organization builds, through a purpose-built platform, an in-house data team, or both.

Three things set genuine inventory intelligence apart from plain reporting:

- **Unified visibility.** A single, current view of stock, demand, and flow across every location, channel, and tier. Not a monthly cross-check pieced together from five systems that were never built to talk to each other.
- **Predictive signal, not just historical record.** Forecasting what demand and stock positions will look like next week and next month, refreshed nonstop. Not only reporting what they were last week on a fixed cadence.
- **Decision-ready output.** Insight a category manager, planner, or supply chain lead can act on directly: a flagged risk, a reorder recommendation, an allocation suggestion. Not raw data that still needs a skilled analyst to interpret first.

This distinction matters in practice. A weekly stock report is reporting. A system flags this three weeks ahead: a specific SKU in a specific region is on track to stock out. It uses current sell-through velocity and replenishment lead time. That's intelligence. The difference is a gap. Finding out you have a problem is one thing. Finding out you're about to have one, while there's still time to act, is another.

Inventory intelligence sits apart from three adjacent ideas that get mixed up with it:

- **General business intelligence.** Flexible dashboards and reporting across any business function. Not built for inventory decisions, and usually needing heavy custom work before they deliver inventory-specific value.
- **Traditional inventory tracking.** Watching stock levels through rule-based logic, such as reordering when stock crosses a threshold. Not predictive, adaptive models that learn as conditions change.
- **ERP and supply chain suite modules.** Inventory features bundled into one much bigger system, typically with heavy setup work that has little to do with inventory decisions specifically.

Real inventory intelligence sits at the crossing point of these three. It is built just for inventory and replenishment decisions. It is predictive and adaptive rather than purely rule-based. And it aims for speed and nonstop learning, not periodic reporting.

## The Scale of the Problem This Discipline Exists to Solve

Let's ground this in the real financial stakes. The case for treating inventory intelligence as a serious priority rests on cost. This isn't a nice-to-have reporting upgrade. The status quo is already costly.

The most current research on this comes from the IHL Group's 2026 Inventory Distortion Study. It puts the combined global cost of stockouts and overstock, what the industry calls inventory distortion, at $1.7 trillion a year. That's roughly 6.2% of global retail sales.

That figure has fallen from a COVID-era peak of 10.4% of retail sales in 2021. That's real, hard-won progress. But the plain-language version is worth sitting with. Retailers still destroy a sum roughly equal to South Korea's entire GDP every year. They lose it on inventory they can't sell and customers they can't serve.

Out-of-stocks account for the larger share of distortion: 65.6%, against 34.4% for overstock. Organizations are, in other words, roughly twice as likely to fail a customer through absence as through excess.

Breaking the total down by cause shows one cause dwarfing everything else. Empty shelves alone account for $690.9 billion a year, more than three times the next-largest driver. The rest splits fairly evenly across both sides of the ledger. On the stockout side: poor service ($165.6B) and misplaced products ($145.2B). On the overstock side: supplier issues ($198.0B), poor buying calls ($148.8B), and spoilage ($100.7B). Spoilage matters especially for FMCG, given how much of the category runs against a real shelf-life clock.

![Bar chart of annual global inventory distortion by cause in 2026, led by empty shelves at $690.9 billion](/blog/inventory-distortion-by-category.png)

Looked at by function rather than cause, the picture sharpens further. Store-level work carries the largest share of total losses, at $734.7 billion. Supply chain gaps account for $619.4 billion. Manufacturer-related distortion adds $355.3 billion.

Here's the hard finding to face. Store-level work, the point furthest from most enterprise forecasting spend, carries the biggest share of loss. A lot of smart upstream planning work is still undone. The damage happens in the last few feet of the supply chain. That's right where unified, real-time visibility tends to be weakest.

## The Problem Looks Different Depending on Where You Operate

It's tempting to treat "$1.7 trillion in global distortion" as one flat problem. It isn't.

![Grouped bar chart of 2026 out-of-stock and overstock losses for North America, EMEA, APAC and LATAM](/blog/inventory-distortion-by-region.png)

APAC carries the biggest total burden of any region. It posts $417 billion in out-of-stock losses and $212 billion in overstock losses, totaling 6.7% of regional retail sales. APAC also posts the slowest progress since 2021, at 23.7%.

LATAM sits at one extreme in one way, and the same extreme in another. It has the highest distortion rate against sales of any region, at 7.5%, despite lower total losses than APAC or EMEA. LATAM is also improving fastest, at 34.7% since 2021.

North America and EMEA both sit in the middle on total burden. EMEA is one of the two global leaders in progress, down 34.3% since 2021.

Here's the practical takeaway for any organization operating across multiple regions. "Biggest problem" and "worst trend" are different questions. Inventory intelligence priorities should reflect both. A region with a large total number but strong progress may need less urgent action than a smaller region that's stuck.
