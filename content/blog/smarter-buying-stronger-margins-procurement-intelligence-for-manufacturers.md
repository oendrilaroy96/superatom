## Procurement is the biggest cost in a factory

In manufacturing, procurement is not a back office task. Almost everything that goes into a product is bought from a supplier: raw materials, components, packaging, energy and spare parts. That makes procurement one of the biggest drivers of cost, margin and delivery in any factory.

The numbers show how big this is. In 2021, US manufacturers spent about $3.3 trillion with suppliers of materials, parts, packaging and fuel. That is roughly two thirds of the $4.9 trillion worth of goods they shipped (NIST). In other words, for many manufacturers, most of every sales dollar goes straight to suppliers.

Because the spend is so large, small changes matter a lot. Take a manufacturer with $500 million in sales that spends two thirds of it with suppliers. Saving just 1% on that spend adds more than $3 million straight to profit. Paying 1% too much takes the same amount away.

And the pressure is rising. Manufacturers expect their input costs to rise by an average of 5.4% over the next year and 89% of companies have faced a supplier risk event in the last five years (Ivalua). 86% of supply chain leaders say tariffs or trade policy have already hit their operations and 45% of manufacturers are passing higher input costs on to their customers (RELEX via Manufacturing.net).

Yet in most factories, procurement still runs on month end reports, emails and spreadsheets. The data needed to make better decisions already exists. It is just scattered across plants and systems. That gap is where margin is lost every day.

## Pain points in manufacturing procurement

A manufacturer buys to a Bill of Materials (BOM), not a catalogue. If one part is missing, the whole product cannot be built. That makes buying for a factory very different from buying for an office.

Here are the six pain points buyers deal with every day, with a simple example of each.

- **Commodity price swings.** A car parts maker agrees a yearly price for steel brackets in January. By April steel prices have jumped and the supplier asks for more money. The buyer has no quick way to check if the new price is fair, so it is usually accepted.
- **Many plants, many systems.** A company runs three plants, each with its own Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) system. All three buy the same bearing from the same supplier, but under three different part codes and at three different prices. Nobody sees the total volume, so nobody negotiates a better deal.
- **Complex BOMs.** A washing machine has hundreds of parts. When engineering changes the motor design through an Engineering Change Order (ECO), the old motors sit unused in the warehouse, open Purchase Orders (POs) keep arriving and the new motor has to be sourced in a hurry.
- **Fragile suppliers.** A small supplier makes one special rubber seal. A fire at its site, or a shortage at its own raw material supplier, stops that seal. Two weeks later the assembly line stops too, because there was no second source.
- **Inventory pressure.** A plant holds six months of packaging film it rarely uses, but runs out of a small sensor it needs every day. Cash is stuck in the wrong stock and production still stops.
- **Tariffs and global sourcing.** A new duty on imported aluminium changes the landed cost of a part overnight. The buyer must decide quickly whether to absorb the cost, pass it on, or move to a local supplier, often without clear numbers.

## The data problem hiding inside every factory

Manufacturers are not short of procurement data. Every purchase, receipt, invoice, stock movement and quality check is already recorded somewhere. The trouble is where it sits and how it is used.

![Table showing the procurement data a manufacturer holds (purchases and prices, parts and demand, stock, supplier performance, contracts, markets and trade), where each usually lives and what it can tell procurement](/blog/procurement-data-table.webp)

Today, most of this data never meets. 79% of manufacturers have no dedicated software to track supplier performance and 74% still rely on spreadsheets (ThoughtMinds). Only 60% of large and 30% of small organisations use a Procure to Pay (P2P) system (McKinsey).

The result is slow decisions. A manual supplier analysis can take around three months (ThoughtMinds) and copper can move 20% in that time. Nearly two thirds of organisations were late responding to supplier risk events because they had no continuous monitoring (Ivalua). Meanwhile, spend managed per procurement Full Time Equivalent (FTE) is 50% higher than five years ago (McKinsey). Buyers spend their day chasing suppliers instead of negotiating with them.

## So what is procurement intelligence?

Think of it as a decision layer that connects with existing systems (ERPs). It pulls together POs, invoices, BOMs, inventory, supplier performance, contracts, commodity indices and tariff data from every plant. Then it does three things spreadsheets cannot:

1. It harmonises. One bearing becomes one bearing everywhere, with one supplier name and one comparable price.
2. It watches. It spots price moves, slipping suppliers and stock risks as they happen, not at month end.
3. It recommends. It tells the Chief Procurement Officer (CPO) and each plant buyer what to do next: push back, find another source, or move stock.

![Diagram of how procurement intelligence works: ERP and MRP, PLM, QMS and WMS and market feeds flow into a unified data layer, then analytics and AI, then dashboards and alerts, then action, with results feeding back so models keep learning](/blog/procurement-intelligence-works.webp)

The hardest step and the most valuable one, is the unified data layer. Once parts and suppliers are harmonised across plants, the savings start to show up quickly.

Most manufacturers move through the same stages. They start fragmented, with plant level spreadsheets and month end reports. They become visible, with one view of spend across all plants. Then they become predictive, with commodity linked cost models, supplier risk scores and stock alerts. The biggest jump in value comes at that third stage, because procurement starts acting before the problem reaches the production line.

## Where the money actually shows up

**Same part, different price.** Joining spend across plants almost always reveals the same component bought at very different prices. That is often the fastest saving in manufacturing procurement.

**Every price increase, tested against the market.** Link each material to its market index, such as LME metals, resin prices, energy and FX and build a should cost for each part. Now your buyer can answer a supplier's price increase request with evidence, not guesswork.

It also works the other way. When the index falls, the system flags suppliers who have not passed the lower price back.

![Flowchart for testing a supplier price increase against the market: the supplier asks for a price increase, the price is compared with the index movement since the base date and if justified by the market it is accepted, if partly justified a counter offer is made with index evidence and if not justified it is rejected and the supplier is flagged for review](/blog/procurement-price-increase-test.webp)

**Suppliers you can see coming.** Score every supplier on OTIF, quality PPM, lead time and outside risk such as financial stress or location. Flag single source parts before they stop a line, not after.

**Working capital released.** Show days of cover for each part in each plant. Move surplus stock between sites instead of buying it again and clear Excess and Obsolete (E&O) stock left behind by engineering changes.

**Contracts that are actually followed.** Check every invoice against agreed prices, index clauses and rebates. One global pharma manufacturer found more than USD 10 million in lost value with an Artificial Intelligence (AI) invoice to contract check built in just four weeks (McKinsey).

**Tariffs modelled before they land.** Attach country of origin and Harmonized System (HS) codes to every critical part. Then landed cost scenarios take minutes instead of weeks.

## Why intelligence matters in manufacturing

In most industries, a bad buying decision costs money. In manufacturing, it can also stop a production line, delay customer orders and lock up cash for months. That is why procurement intelligence matters more here than almost anywhere else.

- **It protects margin.** Direct materials flow straight into the Cost of Goods Sold (COGS). Every price increase that goes unchecked comes straight off the profit line.
- **It keeps lines running.** Seeing a slipping supplier or a coming shortage early is the difference between a quick fix and a line stoppage.
- **It frees up cash.** Knowing exactly what stock sits in every plant lets the business buy less, move more and release working capital.
- **It speeds up decisions.** Buyers answer a price request in hours, not weeks, because the market data, contract terms and supplier history are already in one place.
- **It builds resilience.** With a clear view of single source parts and tariff exposure, manufacturers can plan a second source before a crisis, not during one.
- **It lets people do better work.** Buyers spend less time chasing emails and fixing spreadsheets and more time negotiating and building supplier relationships.

McKinsey estimates procurement can become 25% to 40% more efficient as AI takes over routine work (McKinsey). But AI only works on clean, joined up data. Automating a broken process simply produces errors faster (SysGenPro).

The winners will not be the manufacturers with the most data. They will be the ones that join it fastest and act on it first. So the next time a supplier asks for a price increase, the question is simple: will your team be searching through spreadsheets, or will it already know the answer?
